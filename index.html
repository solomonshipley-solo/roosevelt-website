Theodore Roosevelt · 1858–1919 · 26th President, 1901–1909

America’s Conservation President

Theodore Roosevelt (1858–1919) was the 26th president of the United States (1901–1909) and was responsible for setting aside an estimated 230 million acres of federal lands, including 150 national forests, the first 55 federal bird reservation and game preserves, 5 national parks and the first 18 national monuments. He changed the history of the nation and the environment forever.

Overview

Early Life
and Influences

Roosevelt was a sickly child, plagued by severe asthma attacks, but he learned to cope with his adversities from his father. Nature was important to him in his health and growth. The circumstances of his childhood in wildernesses, his education, and his personal pursuits predisposed him to be an environmental protector.

While he was attending Harvard studying natural history, he gathered natural specimens and while he was elected into the New York State Legislature in 1882, he sent most the Roosevelt Museum of Natural History to the Smithsonian. As a kid he hated living in the North Dakota Badlands, but he loved having the ability to go and hunt on the land and the solitude it provided.

It motivated him so much it defined his philosophy for the rest of his life.

What He Built

Major
Accomplishments

Establishing Protected Lands

In 1905, Roosevelt established the U. S. Forest Service, with Gifford Pinchot as its first Chief Forester. Pinchot’s excellent eye for habitat contributed to the designation of many new valuable forests and wilderness areas.

He and Roosevelt established or expanded 150 national forests; created 51 federal bird (wildlife) refuges; four national game preserves; six national parks; 18 national monuments.

150 national forests · 51 bird refuges · 4 game preserves · 6 parks · 18 monuments

The National Wildlife Refuge System

Inspired by his extraordinary love for his feathery friends, the avid ornithologist and naturalist started a pioneering experiment in March 14th, 1903 with designing habitats for the wildlife he dearly loved that resulted in the establishment of the National Wildlife Refuge System.

It was an experiment that began by protecting one of his much valued rookeries, Pelican Island, Florida for highly endangered pelicans, plus Breton Island, Louisiana, and National Bison Range Montana — perhaps the first wildlife restoration attempts ever by man.

March 14, 1903 · Pelican Island, FL · Breton Island, LA · National Bison Range, MT

Sustainable Resource Management

Of course, Theodore Roosevelt had been affected by the wisdom of earlier proponents of wise-use, including Gifford Pinchot. Roosevelt felt that Nature was supposed to provide for mankind using conserved use. In a conserved wilderness, forest could be logged, activities could be enjoyed and water could be diverted for irrigation.

In 1902, Roosevelt created the federal Reclamation Service. This agency, utilizing dams and irrigation, devised land to grow crops in portions of the countryside that were previously too arid for adequate farming. To date, the Service has developed millions of acres of farmland.

Federal Reclamation Service · established 1902 · millions of acres of farmland

The Idea

Philosophical
Impact

A New Word: “Conservation”

During the first decades of the 20th century, Roosevelt and Gifford Pinchot, his closest adviser on environmental matters, felt a new word was needed to reflect a new era of environmentalism. “Conservation” was the term they chose.

Making it accessible for the public is definitely one of Roosevelt’s key achievements. As a result, Americans developed a quite different appreciation of their bond with nature.

Parks as the Nation’s Lungs

Roosevelt’s vision for parks was also that they act like lungs, absorbing the carbon dioxide released by human activity like burning fossil fuels, because of this reducing global warming. As a changing climate alters their habitat, parks give animals some breathing room to adjust.

A Living Climate Legacy

The land Roosevelt protected serves to combat climate change with its natural carbon sequestration, because of this keeping alive the conservation legacy of Roosevelt in the coming decades.

The Other Side of the Coin

The Native American aspect cannot be forgotten and it’s the other side of the coin that Roosevelt took as well. For example, during his presidency nearly 86 million acres of Indian lands were handed over to the National Forests, which is to say, more or less the whole reservation territory of the Native Americans.

So, one gets the picture of Roosevelt and his conservationists as people who were more concerned about the wilderness itself than they were about the people living in it.

The Blueprint

Theodore Roosevelt’s environmental policies laid the foundation for the country’s approach to land and resource use, a legacy that also plays a part in shaping and influencing contemporary global conservation actions in many ways.

Through the balance of resource preservation and practical use, the way he thought the country should deal with its natural resources continues serving as a blueprint that is being studied and followed after more than a hundred years.
